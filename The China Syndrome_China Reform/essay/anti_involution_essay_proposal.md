# Short Essay Proposal

**Course:** *The China Syndrome: Modern Reforms in China's Economy and Their Global Implications*  
**Proposed essay length:** 5–10 pages  
**Proposed topic:** Chinese industrial reform, overcapacity, and global spillovers  
**Working title:** **Anti-Involution and Exported Deflation: China’s New Supply-Side Reform and the Global Spillovers of Green Industrial Overcapacity**

---

## 1. Motivation and Research Puzzle

China’s industrial policy has achieved remarkable scale in strategic manufacturing sectors such as electric vehicles (EVs), lithium-ion batteries, and solar photovoltaic products. These sectors are central to China’s ambition to move from high-speed growth to “high-quality development” and to dominate the next generation of green manufacturing. Yet the same model has also produced a new policy problem: excessive local investment, duplicated capacity, aggressive price wars, falling margins, and external trade tensions.

This tension is now captured in China’s policy language as **“anti-involution”**. In July 2025, a State Council executive meeting reviewed measures to strengthen market-order regulation in the new-energy vehicle sector.[^1] The broader policy context is also linked to China’s push for a unified national market, which aims to reduce local protectionism, regional barriers, and unfair competition.[^2] These policy signals suggest that Beijing is not abandoning industrial policy, but trying to discipline the excessive competition and overinvestment generated by the industrial-policy state itself.

The global implication is important. If China’s domestic market cannot absorb expanded production, firms may push excess output abroad through lower export prices. This creates a possible **“China Shock 2.0”**: not the earlier shock of labor-intensive manufacturing exports, but a new wave of clean-tech and advanced-manufacturing exports that may transmit Chinese overcapacity and price pressure to foreign markets.

---

## 2. Research Question

**Main research question:**  
**Can China’s anti-involution campaign restrain industrial overcapacity without exporting deflation to the rest of the world?**

### Sub-questions

1. Why does “involution-style competition” arise within China’s industrial-policy model?
2. Why are sectors such as EVs, batteries, and solar panels especially exposed to overcapacity and price wars?
3. Does simple open trade data show a pattern consistent with exported deflation — rising export quantities and falling export unit values?
4. Is anti-involution likely to become a genuine structural reform, or mainly an administrative correction of symptoms?

---

## 3. Core Argument / Tentative Thesis

The essay will argue that China’s anti-involution campaign represents a **new supply-side reform within the industrial-policy state**, rather than a liberal market reform. It aims to preserve China’s manufacturing scale and technological upgrading while limiting the negative consequences of excessive local competition, duplicated investment, and destructive price wars.

However, the campaign is unlikely to fully solve the problem unless it is accompanied by deeper reforms to local-government incentives, domestic consumption, private-sector confidence, and the fiscal structure that encourages local governments to chase industrial projects. Without such reforms, China may continue to absorb overcapacity externally through exports. In that case, anti-involution may reduce some domestic price wars, but the global economy will still face Chinese export-price pressure, trade disputes, and defensive industrial-policy responses.

---

## 4. Conceptual Mechanism

The essay will use the following causal logic:

1. **Central industrial priorities** identify strategic sectors, such as EVs, batteries, solar PV, and advanced manufacturing.
2. **Local governments compete** to attract firms, investment, subsidies, land, credit, and supply-chain projects.
3. **Firms expand capacity rapidly**, often because scale, market share, and policy access matter more than short-term profitability.
4. **Domestic demand is insufficient** to absorb output at profitable prices.
5. **Price wars and margin compression** emerge within China.
6. **Export markets absorb part of the pressure**, leading to rising export quantities and falling export unit values.
7. **Foreign governments respond** through tariffs, anti-subsidy investigations, local-content rules, and their own industrial policies.

This mechanism links China’s domestic reform problem to its global implications.

---

## 5. Relationship to Existing Literature

The essay will be anchored in three strands of literature.

### 5.1 Industrial policy and the limits of subsidy-led upgrading

A useful academic benchmark is Branstetter and Li’s NBER paper on *Made in China 2025*. Using listed-firm data and a difference-in-differences approach, they study how the policy affected subsidies, R&D expenditure, patenting, productivity, and profitability.[^3] Their finding that targeted firms received more subsidies without clear evidence of broad productivity or profitability gains provides a strong foundation for this essay. It suggests that industrial policy can expand scale without necessarily generating proportional efficiency gains.

### 5.2 Overcapacity in green manufacturing

The solar sector offers a clear example. The OECD argues that growing production capacity and low utilization have contributed to imbalances in the solar sector, depressing prices and firms’ profitability.[^4] This supports the essay’s claim that China’s green industrial strategy has generated both global cost reductions and serious market distortions.

### 5.3 Macroeconomic rebalancing and external spillovers

The IMF’s 2025 Article IV report on China highlights weak domestic demand, deflationary pressures, and continued reliance on exports as important macroeconomic risks with adverse spillovers to trading partners.[^5] The World Bank similarly emphasizes the need for reforms that strengthen domestic demand, social protection, private investment, and local-government finance.[^6] These sources help connect anti-involution to the broader question of China’s growth-model rebalancing.

## 6. Empirical Component: Simple, Feasible, and Open-Data Based

The essay will not attempt a full causal econometric paper. Given the 5–10 page limit, the empirical part will be used only to provide **descriptive evidence consistent with the argument**.

### 6.1 Data sources

The empirical section can use only open data:

| Data source | Variables | Use in essay |
|---|---|---|
| UN Comtrade | Export value, quantity, reporter, partner, HS product code, year/month | Construct export quantity and export unit values by product and destination.[^7] |
| World Bank WITS | Trade values, tariffs, partner/product breakdowns | Alternative access point for Comtrade-style trade data and tariff information.[^8] |
| China Customs / GACC | China customs statistics by commodity and partner | Cross-check official Chinese trade trends if needed.[^9] |
| National Bureau of Statistics of China | PPI, industrial profits, costs, inventories | Background evidence on domestic price/profit pressure.[^10] |

### 6.2 Product scope

The preferred sample is a narrow “new three” clean-tech sample:

| Sector | HS code | Definition / rationale |
|---|---:|---|
| Battery electric vehicles | 870380 | Vehicles with only electric motor for propulsion.[^11] |
| Lithium-ion batteries | 850760 | Lithium-ion electric accumulators.[^12] |
| Solar PV cells/modules | 854142, 854143, older 854140 | Photovoltaic cells not assembled / assembled in modules or panels; older data often require 854140 because of HS revisions.[^13] |

The essay can compare these sectors with a small set of less policy-sensitive manufactured goods as a rough control group, but this is optional. For a short essay, the cleanest approach is to focus only on the three core sectors and interpret the results descriptively.

### 6.3 Key variable: export unit value

The main variable is:

\[
UnitValue_{p,d,t} = \frac{ExportValue_{p,d,t}}{ExportQuantity_{p,d,t}}
\]

where \(p\) is product, \(d\) is destination, and \(t\) is year or month.

A falling unit value does not perfectly measure price because product quality, composition, reporting units, and exchange-rate effects may change. However, for a short essay, unit values are a standard and transparent proxy for export-price pressure.

### 6.4 Descriptive figures

The essay can include two simple figures:

**Figure 1:** China’s export quantity index for EVs, lithium-ion batteries, and solar PV products, 2017–2025 or 2021–2025.  
**Figure 2:** China’s export unit-value index for the same products and period.

The expected pattern is:

- export quantities rise sharply;
- export unit values decline or weaken;
- the pattern is strongest in sectors associated with overcapacity and price-war debates.

### 6.5 Optional simple regression

If a regression is included, it should be deliberately simple:

\[
\Delta \log(UnitValue_{p,d,t}) = \beta \Delta \log(ExportQuantity_{p,d,t}) + \alpha_p + \alpha_d + \alpha_t + \varepsilon_{p,d,t}
\]

where:

- \(UnitValue_{p,d,t}\) is China’s export unit value for product \(p\) to destination \(d\) in year/month \(t\);
- \(ExportQuantity_{p,d,t}\) is the corresponding export quantity;
- \(\alpha_p\), \(\alpha_d\), and \(\alpha_t\) are product, destination, and time fixed effects.

**Expected sign:** \(\beta < 0\).  
This would mean that faster export quantity growth is associated with falling unit values, consistent with exported price pressure.

**Important limitation:** this regression is not causal. It does not prove that overcapacity causes lower export prices. It only tests whether the observed trade pattern is consistent with the essay’s mechanism.

## 7. Proposed Essay Structure

### Section 1. Introduction: the puzzle of successful but excessive industrial policy

The introduction will present the central paradox: China’s industrial policy has successfully created globally competitive green-manufacturing sectors, but it has also generated overcapacity, price wars, deflationary pressure, and trade frictions. The section will introduce anti-involution as Beijing’s response and state the thesis.

### Section 2. What is anti-involution?

This section will define “involution-style competition” as excessive internal competition that reduces profitability without necessarily improving productivity. It will connect the term to China’s 2025 policy focus on regulating NEV competition and building a unified national market.

### Section 3. The political economy of overcapacity

This section will explain why overcapacity is not accidental. It emerges from the interaction of central industrial priorities, local-government competition, financial support, industrial parks, and firm incentives to expand scale. The section will emphasize that local governments are not merely implementing central plans; they also compete against one another, often creating duplicated investment.

### Section 4. Why EVs, batteries, and solar matter

This section will use the “new three” sectors as compact case evidence. These sectors are ideal because they show both sides of China’s model: rapid technological scaling and cost reduction, but also falling margins, global dependence, and foreign trade concerns. The OECD solar report will provide sector-specific support.

### Section 5. Open-data empirical illustration

This section will present one or two descriptive figures and, if space permits, one simple regression. The goal is not to claim causality, but to show whether China’s clean-tech exports display the pattern predicted by the argument: expanding export volumes and weakening unit values.

### Section 6. Can anti-involution work?

This section will evaluate the policy. Anti-involution may reduce destructive price wars by encouraging consolidation, regulating competition, and disciplining local governments. However, it faces structural constraints: weak household demand, local fiscal pressure, and the continued importance of industrial upgrading as a growth strategy.

### Section 7. Global implications

This section will discuss two possible outcomes. If anti-involution succeeds, China may stabilize prices and reduce some trade frictions, but global green-technology prices may fall more slowly. If it fails, China will continue exporting overcapacity, intensifying trade disputes and defensive industrial policy abroad.

### Section 8. Conclusion

The conclusion will restate the argument: anti-involution is not a retreat from China’s industrial-policy model, but a corrective mechanism inside it. Its success depends on whether China can shift from production maximization toward productivity, profitability, and domestic demand.

---

## 8. Expected Contribution

The essay’s contribution is to connect three debates that are often discussed separately:

1. China’s domestic supply-side reform and anti-involution policy;
2. the political economy of local-government competition and overcapacity;
3. global spillovers through clean-tech exports and falling unit values.

The essay will be short, but it can still be original because it treats anti-involution not simply as a slogan, but as a window into the contradiction of China’s contemporary growth model.

---

## 9. Limitations and Scope Control

The essay will avoid making claims that are too strong for a short paper. In particular:

- it will not claim to identify a causal effect of anti-involution policy;
- it will not attempt to measure firm-level subsidies directly;
- it will not cover all sectors affected by involution;
- it will focus on EVs, batteries, and solar PV because they are internationally important and empirically observable in open trade data;
- the empirical section will be used only as descriptive support for the main political-economy argument.
