\section{Tensions and Outlook}

Anti-involution targets the wrong end of the problem. It can discipline the price competition that drives margins below zero, but insufficient domestic demand, the deeper issue, stays untouched. Raising quality thresholds and facilitating consolidation does not create buyers.

The most likely trajectory is a muddled middle. Solar manufacturing may see partial rationalization as small firms exit, but the EV sector is harder to consolidate because local governments hold stakes in regional automakers. No official wants to close a factory that employs constituents and generates tax revenue, even if the factory cannot turn a profit. Capacity additions already in the pipeline will arrive even as demand growth slows. On the external side, trade responses will fragment the export market without eliminating it: the United States has opted for high tariff walls while much of the Global South remains open to Chinese imports on price grounds alone. Chinese firms will redirect exports toward whichever markets remain accessible.

What would genuine structural reform look like? It would require raising the household share of national income. That means expanding social insurance to reduce precautionary saving, and reforming the fiscal system so that local governments depend less on land sales and industrial project revenue. It also means allowing real interest rates to rise, so that household savings earn a market return rather than being channeled at repressed rates into industrial investment. Each reform is politically difficult because each redistributes income away from the state sector and toward households. Anti-involution asks none of these questions.

Yet the campaign does reveal something about the state's capacity for self-correction. The same apparatus that created globally competitive green-manufacturing sectors now attempts to regulate the overcapacity it produced. That is not retreat. It is the industrial-policy state turning its own tools inward, and the fact that Beijing recognizes the problem at all is itself informative. The question is whether recognition leads to reform or merely to management. The precedents are not encouraging: China has discussed rebalancing toward consumption since at least the Twelfth Five-Year Plan (2011--2015), and the household consumption share of GDP has barely moved.

I see two indicators that would undermine this assessment: a sustained rise in China's household consumption share of GDP, or convergence of capacity utilization in the ``new three'' toward international norms. Neither is visible in recent data.
