\section{Tensions and Outlook}

Anti-involution targets the wrong end of the problem. The shortfall in domestic demand is the deeper issue, and quality thresholds and merger facilitation do nothing about that.

On the domestic front, the most likely trajectory is a muddled middle. Solar manufacturing may see some rationalization as small firms exit, but the EV sector is harder to consolidate because local governments hold stakes in regional automakers and no official wants to shut a factory that employs constituents.

The more interesting adjustment channel is overseas. Chinese EV supply-chain firms invested more abroad than at home for the first time in 2024 \citep{IEA2025GEO}, and industry estimates put overseas manufacturing capacity roughly doubling by 2026. Part of this is tariff-driven: companies are localizing production to get around trade barriers. But if Chinese firms relocate a meaningful share of their capacity to Southeast Asia and Latin America, they could endogenously relieve some of the domestic overcapacity that anti-involution was supposed to address.

But relocating factories is not the same as fixing the underlying problem. The fiscal incentives inside China that push local officials to build capacity have not changed at all.

If involution remains unresolved domestically, the consequences extend beyond trade. Persistent involutionary competition erodes firm profitability and drags down aggregate productivity, creating a vicious cycle in which weak growth reinforces the conditions for further involution. Youth labour-market prospects deteriorate, reducing social mobility and feeding the disengagement that Chinese commentators call ``lying flat.'' And by compressing margins across entire sectors, involution discourages the risk-taking that industrial upgrading requires.

Genuine structural reform would mean raising the household share of national income: expanded social insurance, fiscal restructuring away from land-sale dependence, relaxation of financial repression. If the government invests excessive resources in ``dividing the pie well'' but the speed of ``making the pie bigger'' cannot keep pace, China may ultimately face the risk of falling into ``common poverty.'' Long-term weak domestic demand may become normalized, making China's attempt to counter external pressure by ``strengthening the domestic circulation'' highly challenging. Anti-involution does not ask any of these questions.

The precedents are not encouraging. China has been talking about rebalancing toward consumption since the 12th Five-Year Plan (2011--2015), and the household consumption share of GDP has barely moved. What I see is a Chinese industrial sector that has learned to work around the constraints it faces, finding new outlets for overcapacity while leaving the underlying causes in place.
