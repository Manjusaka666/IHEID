\section{Overcapacity in the ``New Three''}

Table~\ref{tab:overcapacity} gives a snapshot of capacity pressure in the three sectors.

\paragraph{Solar PV.}
China's solar PV manufacturing capacity exceeds 1~TW per year. Global installations in 2024 reached roughly 600~GW \citep{IEAPVPS2025}. Even if every single panel installed on Earth were Chinese-made, nearly half the factory floor would be idle. Chinese firms hold more than 80\% of global output at every major stage from polysilicon through module assembly. The OECD's MAGIC database identifies solar as the most heavily subsidized sector it tracks: government support to solar firms averaged 3.2\% of revenue, compared with 0.9\% across all sectors \citep{OECD2026Solar, OECD2025Subsidies}.

\paragraph{Lithium-ion batteries.}
China hosts about 85\% of global battery cell manufacturing capacity \citep{USCC2025, IEA2025GEO}. Batteries are an intermediate good whose demand depends on downstream adoption of EVs and grid storage. The bull case is that global battery demand will more than triple by 2030. But battery pack prices in China fell about 30\% in 2024, against 10--15\% in Europe and the United States \citep{IEA2025GEO}. If this were a global learning curve, the price drops would look similar everywhere. Chinese prices fell roughly twice as fast, which looks more like a domestic glut than shared technological progress.

\paragraph{Electric vehicles.}
The EV sector is different again, and politically the most visible case. Because the Chinese government regards electric vehicles as an emerging industry in which China has the potential to gain global dominance, it has mobilized national resources to support the sector. According to estimates by the Center for Strategic and International Studies, from 2009 to 2023, China's electric vehicle industry received approximately USD 230.9 billion in government subsidies. The main form of subsidy has been the vehicle purchase tax exemption policy, while direct consumer purchase subsidies were phased out at the end of 2022. Because automobiles are a consumer good with strong local-employment ties, provincial governments also have direct stakes in keeping regional automakers alive. Under these large-scale subsidies, the industry has become severely involuted, with wave after wave of price wars. Li Shufu, chairman of Geely Holding Group, criticized the industry as having ``the highest degree of involution in the world.'' NBS data show that automobile manufacturing PPI fell 2.8\% in 2025, slightly worse than the 2.6\% decline for industry as a whole \citep{NBS2026PPI}. China's domestic market cannot absorb the massive excess capacity driven by subsidies: nearly 20 million units of excess new energy vehicle capacity are expected by 2025, while global new energy vehicle sales in 2024 were only 17.76 million units and China's market sales were 11.5 million units \citep{IEA2025GEO, MayrEtAl2026}. The European Parliament study documents a rising share of loss-making firms in the automobile sector \citep{MayrEtAl2026}. In China's EV sector, the soft budget constraint described in Section~1 keeps loss-making firms alive, and the price floor that exit would establish never materializes.

Is this really overcapacity, or forward-looking investment? Beijing says global green demand will eventually absorb the surplus. The OECD and IMF disagree, treating the gap as structural and rooted in the subsidy architecture of Section~1 \citep{IMF2026, OECD2025Subsidies}. I side with the structural interpretation. Capacity investment follows the incentives of local officials and state lenders, not any firm's assessment of future demand. Calling it ``forward-looking'' imposes a market rationality on a process that the tournament mechanism overrides.

In all three sectors, a fiscal and financial architecture prevents the surplus from correcting itself.
