\section{Global Spillover: Downward Price Pressure}

\subsection*{China Shock 2.0}

When \citet{AutorDornHanson2013} documented the original China Shock, the story was relatively simple: WTO accession accelerated a trade shock in labor-intensive manufacturing, and American workers in competing industries bore the cost. Two decades on, the shock has returned in a different form. What China now exports at scale is capital-intensive green technology, the ``new three.''

China has genuine advantages in engineering talent and scale, but the policy apparatus from Section~1 amplified those advantages with directed capital, so the current shock layers policy-driven capacity expansion on top of real cost competitiveness. The twist is that cheap solar panels and batteries slow climate change, which makes the welfare calculus genuinely hard.

\subsection*{Strategic entry versus residual exports}

Not all Chinese green-tech exports reflect the same logic. Some firms planned for export markets early, before domestic margins collapsed. BYD building plants in Thailand and Hungary is deliberate market entry. Other firms are redirecting output abroad because the domestic price war has left them unable to sell at home, closer to what economists call loss-tolerant export competition: margins below long-run average cost, sustained by soft budget constraints. \subsection*{Evidence of downward price pressure}

Chinese export volumes in clean-technology sectors have risen sharply since 2022 while unit values have fallen \citep{USCC2025}. \citet{RotunnoRuta2024} provide econometric evidence that Chinese industrial subsidies are associated with higher export volumes and lower export prices in subsidized sectors.

A lot of commentary calls this ``exported deflation,'' which is the wrong framing. The decline is in the relative price of clean-technology goods, not in the general price level. \subsection*{The green-public-goods counterargument}

The strongest objection is that cheap solar panels and batteries speed up global decarbonization, especially in developing countries that cannot afford unsubsidized clean technology. For much of Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, affordable Chinese solar modules are the difference between decarbonized electrification and staying on fossil fuels.

Yet adjustment costs concentrate: workers in European solar-module assembly or American EV-component manufacturing bear losses while benefits spread thinly across consumers. The United States imposed a 100\% tariff on Chinese EVs in 2024 \citep{USTR2024}. The European Union adopted countervailing duties, 17.0\% for BYD up to 35.3\% for SAIC \citep{EC2024BEV}. No major economy has taken the subsidized goods and compensated displaced workers at home. The welfare benefit of subsidized overcapacity requires open markets, and overcapacity is closing them.
