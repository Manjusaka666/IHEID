\section{Anti-Involution as Domestic Response}

\subsection{The policy and its instruments}

``Involution-style competition'' (\textit{neijuan shi jingzheng}) describes a pattern in which firms compete on price and scale alone, compressing margins without generating productivity gains. No firm gains a durable advantage because competitors match each price cut: the equilibrium is a race to the bottom in which every participant loses margin and no one gains market power. The result is value destruction rather than creative destruction. Anti-involution is Beijing's label for this pathology, and its policy response.

Central authorities moved to address involution through administrative intervention in 2025. In July, a State Council executive meeting on the new-energy vehicle industry called for curbing ``irrational competition'' and announced strengthened cost surveys and price monitoring across the NEV supply chain \citep{StateCouncil2025NEV}. In January 2025, the NDRC issued guidelines for building a unified national market, targeting local protectionism and regional market barriers that fragment domestic demand and enable duplicated capacity \citep{StateCouncil2025Market}. Specific instruments include tighter safety and quality standards (revised EV standards take effect in 2026) and merger facilitation. Local-government performance evaluation is to shift from output quantity toward quality metrics. Each instrument operates within the existing industrial-policy apparatus: the state is regulating competition, not marketizing it.

\subsection{The MITI analogy and its limits}

A serious counterargument holds that anti-involution represents genuine policy learning: a maturation from quantity-maximizing industrial policy toward quality-focused upgrading. The analogy to Japan's MITI is tempting. During the 1960s and 1970s, MITI used administrative guidance to curb ``excessive competition'' (\textit{kato kyoso}) in steel and automobiles, facilitating consolidation that contributed to subsequent technological upgrading \citep{Johnson1982}.

But did MITI succeed because of administrative coordination alone? Japan's industrial consolidation coincided with one of the fastest sustained increases in household living standards in economic history. Wage-bargaining institutions spread the gains of industrialization, and rising domestic incomes absorbed much of what industry produced. The demand side of the economy was doing the heavy lifting: MITI could consolidate supply because demand was growing fast enough to make the consolidated firms profitable. China faces the opposite. Household consumption remains around 39--40\% of GDP, below the average for upper-middle-income economies and far below advanced-economy benchmarks \citep{WorldBank2025}. The household savings rate sits at approximately 34\%, against a global average of roughly 24\% \citep{MERICS2025}. Financial repression and the systematic channeling of income toward investment compress the household share of national income. The property-sector adjustment since 2021 has eroded household wealth, and limited social protection reinforces precautionary saving. As \citet{KleinPettis2020} argue, these are features of the growth model, not incidental frictions: the model suppresses consumption by design.

This is the core difficulty. Supply-side consolidation only works when the demand side is healthy enough to sustain the surviving firms at profitable scale. Anti-involution may slow the price war among EV manufacturers, but it cannot create the domestic buyers those manufacturers need. Consolidating producers accomplishes little if the consolidated firms still face a market too small to absorb their output. The textbook prescription would be to raise the household share of income through fiscal transfers and expanded social insurance, coupled with a relaxation of financial repression. None of these appear in the anti-involution policy toolkit.

Scale makes matters worse. MITI coordinated a handful of firms in a few sectors. China's overcapacity spans hundreds of cities and thousands of firms across multiple layers of government with conflicting objectives \citep{MERICS2025}. The coordination problem is not incrementally larger; it is qualitatively different. The fiscal architecture of the state also works against compliance. Local governments depend on land-transfer fees and industrial-project revenue for a large share of their discretionary budgets. The tournament mechanism documented in Section~1 persists because the fiscal incentive to attract investment persists. Anti-involution punishes the symptom (destructive price competition) without reforming the incentive structure that generates overcapacity. This creates a familiar principal-agent problem: Beijing issues the directive, but the agents who must implement it are the same local officials whose budgets depend on the investment the directive is trying to restrain. As long as local officials gain revenue and career advancement from attracting new capacity, compliance with anti-involution directives will be selective at best.

I therefore read anti-involution as an administrative correction, not a structural reform. It may reduce the worst price wars through tighter standards and managed consolidation, but it does not address the root cause: production capacity that exceeds domestic demand, sustained by a growth model that favors investment over consumption. The surplus that anti-involution cannot absorb domestically does not disappear. It shows up in export markets.
