\section{Anti-Involution as Domestic Response}

\subsection*{The policy and its instruments}

``Involution-style competition'' (\textit{neijuan shi jingzheng}) is regarded in China as a highly negative sociocultural phenomenon. It refers specifically to a social predicament in which, within a closed space where resources are fixed or scarce, individuals are forced to participate in excessive and irrational competition, ultimately forming a situation in which there is little hope yet no way to withdraw from the competition. In industrial terms, it means firms compete on price and scale alone, cutting margins without ever gaining a durable advantage. Every price cut gets matched, and the equilibrium is a race to the bottom where value is destroyed rather than created. In July 2024, Chinese authorities began to recognize the impact of involution on China's economy. At important meetings such as the Central Politburo meeting, the Central Economic Work Conference, the government's Two Sessions report, and meetings of the Central Financial and Economic Affairs Commission, they proposed measures such as ``strengthening industry self-discipline to prevent involutionary vicious competition'' and ``comprehensively regulating involutionary competition and standardizing the behavior of local governments and enterprises.''

Central authorities moved on two tracks. The first is administrative. On July 1, 2025, Xi Jinping chaired the sixth meeting of the Central Financial and Economic Affairs Commission, where he proposed further advancing the construction of a unified national market and lawfully regulating low-price, disorderly competition among enterprises, releasing the highest-level policy signal on anti-involution. A State Council meeting later that month on the new-energy vehicle industry called for curbing ``irrational competition'' and announced strengthened cost surveys and price monitoring across the NEV supply chain \citep{StateCouncil2025NEV}. Earlier that year the NDRC issued guidelines for a unified national market, going after local protectionism and the regional barriers that fragment domestic demand and allow duplicated capacity to survive \citep{StateCouncil2025Market}. Other instruments include tighter safety and quality standards (revised EV standards take effect in 2026), merger facilitation, and a shift in how local officials get evaluated: less output quantity, more quality metrics.

The second track is more market-oriented, at least in design. On June 30, 2025, the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress passed the revised Anti-Unfair Competition Law, requiring that platform operators must not force or covertly force merchants on their platforms to follow their pricing rules and sell goods below cost, thereby disrupting market competition order. It also requires large enterprises not to abuse their advantages in capital, technology, transaction channels, industry influence, and other areas by forcing small and medium-sized enterprises to accept obviously unreasonable transaction terms, such as payment periods, payment methods, conditions, and liability for breach of contract. A concurrent Pricing Law overhaul aims to establish legal liability for predatory pricing. I think these provisions matter. If enforced, they would put a rule-of-law floor beneath pricing behavior that administrative instruments alone cannot provide. However, the Anti-Unfair Competition Law still does not confront the institutional roots that cause involution, namely the continued existence of local governments' ``gray subsidies'' and GDP-based assessment incentives. As long as this institutional distortion is not eliminated, local governments will continue to drive overcapacity, and vicious competition will be difficult to eradicate.

\subsection*{The MITI analogy and its limits}

A serious counterargument says anti-involution is genuine policy learning, something like what Japan's MITI achieved. MITI used administrative guidance in the 1960s and 1970s to curb ``excessive competition'' (\textit{kato kyoso}) in steel and automobiles, and the resulting consolidation contributed to technological upgrading \citep{Johnson1982}. So why not read anti-involution as a similar step? Japan's industrial consolidation coincided with one of the fastest sustained increases in household living standards in economic history. MITI could consolidate supply because domestic incomes were rising fast enough to make the consolidated firms profitable. China faces the opposite situation. Household consumption sits at 39--40\% of GDP, well below advanced-economy norms \citep{WorldBank2025}. The household savings rate is around 34\%, against a global average near 24\% \citep{MERICS2025}. \citet{KleinPettis2020} argue that financial repression and the channeling of income toward investment are structural features of the growth model. The growth model holds consumption down.

Anti-involution may slow the price war among EV manufacturers, but it cannot create the domestic buyers they need.

MITI also dealt with a handful of firms. China's overcapacity spans hundreds of cities and thousands of firms \citep{MERICS2025}. Beijing issues the directive, but the people who must carry it out are the same local officials whose budgets depend on the investment the directive is trying to restrain.

I read anti-involution as primarily a correction within the existing system. It combines administrative discipline with emerging legal instruments, but it leaves the demand side alone. Persistent involutionary competition harms corporate profitability and the healthy development of entire industries, causing large amounts of resources to be consumed in inefficient markets. Anti-involution may damp the worst price wars, but the underlying situation, production capacity exceeding domestic demand in a growth model that favors investment over consumption, stays as it is. Chinese companies have been forced to export domestic vicious involutionary competition into overseas markets, triggering an escalation of international trade protectionism and posing challenges to global economic stability.
