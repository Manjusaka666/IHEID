# Essay Design Spec: Anti-Involution and Exported Deflation

**Course:** MINT427 — The China Syndrome (Geneva Graduate Institute, Spring 2025-2026)
**Title:** Anti-Involution and Exported Deflation: China's New Supply-Side Reform and the Global Spillovers of Green Industrial Overcapacity
**Format:** LaTeX, EB Garamond 12pt, single space, `natbib` (authoryear, round)
**Target length:** 8-9 pages (hard ceiling: 10 pages including bibliography)
**File structure:** `main.tex` + `section1.txt` through `section5.txt` + `references.bib`

---

## Thesis

Anti-involution is a new supply-side reform *within* the industrial-policy state, not a retreat from it. It aims to discipline the excessive competition and overcapacity generated by China's own industrial-policy apparatus. However, without deeper demand-side structural reform — household consumption, local fiscal restructuring, social protection — anti-involution may reduce domestic price wars while the global economy continues to absorb Chinese overcapacity through downward export-price pressure.

---

## Structure and Word Budgets

Total body: ~4,300 words + table. Bibliography: 15-18 references (~0.8-1.0 pages).

### Section 1: Introduction and the Policy Engine (~1,000 words, ~1.8 pages)

**Purpose:** Present the paradox, state the thesis, and lay out the causal mechanism.

**Opening hook:** China's clean-tech sectors (EVs, batteries, solar PV) are the most successful industrial-policy outcomes of the 21st century — and also the most destabilizing.

**Thesis statement:** Anti-involution is supply-side reform within the industrial-policy state. Without demand-side reform, it disciplines domestic price wars while exporting downward price pressure abroad.

**Causal mechanism (5 links):**
1. Central government targets strategic sectors (MIC 2025, "new three")
2. Local governments compete via subsidies, land, credit, industrial parks — tournament-style political competition (Fang, Li & Wu 2022; connect explicitly to course frameworks on regionally decentralized authoritarianism)
3. **Soft budget constraint**: state-controlled banks and local government financing vehicles absorb losses that would kill firms in a hard-budget-constraint environment — this is what distinguishes state-directed overcapacity from normal business-cycle overinvestment
4. Firms expand for scale/market share rather than profitability; subsidies do not generate proportional productivity gains (Branstetter & Li 2022; Branstetter, Li & Ren 2022)
5. Domestic demand insufficient to absorb output → price wars → export pressure

**Sources:** Fang, Li & Lu (NBER 2025) on policy diffusion; Fang, Li & Wu (NBER 2022) on tournament competition; Branstetter & Li (2022); IMF WP 2024/180 (Rotunno & Ruta).

**Transition to Section 2:** The mechanism predicts overcapacity in centrally targeted sectors. The "New Three" — EVs, batteries, solar PV — provide the test case.

**Design note:** Section ends with the theoretical prediction. Does NOT present empirical evidence (that is Section 2's job).

### Section 2: Overcapacity in the "New Three" (~900 words + table, ~1.7 pages)

**Purpose:** Demonstrate that the mechanism from Section 1 is empirically operative. Domestic capacity/demand data only (trade flow data belongs in Section 4).

**Opens directly with evidence** — no restatement of the mechanism.

**Sector-by-sector evidence (1-2 paragraphs each):**

- **Solar PV:** Manufacturing capacity exceeds 1 TW/year, roughly double global demand. Module prices fell >50% (2023-2025). China holds 80%+ market share across the value chain. OECD (2026) documents solar as the most subsidized sector in the MAGIC database (3.2% of revenue vs. 0.9% average).
- **Lithium-ion batteries:** China produces ~85% of global battery cells. 2024 capacity ~2,500 GWh vs. ~1,100 GWh global demand. Projected 6,300 GWh by 2030.
- **EVs:** Share of loss-making firms nearly doubled from 12% (2014) to >22% (2024). Domestic price wars intensified following subsidy phase-out. NBS PPI data shows persistent producer-price deflation (-2.6% full-year 2025).

**Summary table:**

| Sector | China Capacity | Global Demand (approx.) | Utilization (approx.) | Price Trend (2023-2025) |
|--------|---------------|------------------------|-----------------------|------------------------|
| Solar PV | >1 TW/yr | ~500 GW | ~50% | >50% decline |
| Li-ion Batteries | ~2,500 GWh | ~1,100 GWh | ~44% | ~40% decline |
| EVs | ~40M units/yr | ~22M units | ~55% | ~30% avg decline |

*Table note: Utilization rates are sensitive to denominator definition (nameplate vs. effective capacity). Figures use nameplate capacity from OECD (2026), IMF (2026), and USCC (2025). See sources for methodological details.*

**Contested definition (2 sentences, not a full paragraph):** Beijing argues global green demand will eventually absorb this capacity; the OECD and IMF treat it as structural overcapacity rooted in the subsidy architecture. This essay follows the structural interpretation but acknowledges the demand-potential counterargument.

**Sources:** OECD (2026) solar report; IMF Article IV (2026); USCC Chapter 8 (2025); NBS PPI data; MERICS (2025).

### Section 3: Anti-Involution as Domestic Response (~1,100 words, ~2.0 pages)

**Purpose:** Define anti-involution, describe the policy instruments, and assess whether it constitutes structural reform. This is the analytical core of the essay — the section where the thesis is argued most directly.

**Part A — Definition (~200 words):**
Define "involution-style competition" (neijuan shi jingzheng, 内卷式竞争) as excessive internal competition that compresses margins without improving productivity. Connect to the mechanism: when firms compete on price/scale rather than innovation, the result is value destruction, not value creation.

**Part B — Policy instruments (~300 words):**
- July 2025 State Council executive meeting on NEV industry competition: "curbing irrational competition," strengthening cost survey and price monitoring
- Unified national market guidelines (NDRC, Jan 2025): eliminating local protectionism and regional barriers
- Specific instruments: tighter safety/quality standards (new EV standards effective 2026), market-driven consolidation, shifting local government KPIs from quantity to quality
- These are administrative tools within the industrial-policy apparatus, not market-liberalizing reforms

**Part C — Assessment: the strongest counterargument and rebuttal (~600 words):**

*Counterargument (engage seriously):* Some analysts argue anti-involution represents genuine policy learning — a maturation from quantity-maximizing industrial policy to quality-focused upgrading. Historical precedent: Japan's MITI-era administrative guidance to reduce "excessive competition" (kato kyoso) facilitated consolidation and upgrading in some sectors.

*Rebuttal:* The Chinese case is structurally different from Japan's 1960s-70s experience because:
1. **Demand-side gap:** Japan's capacity consolidation coincided with rapid household income growth that absorbed output. China faces the opposite: weak household consumption, excess savings (~34% vs. 24% global average), property-sector adjustment compressing household wealth, and an absence of robust social protection that drives precautionary saving.
2. **Local fiscal incentives:** The fiscal structure channels revenue to investment rather than transfers. Local governments depend on land sales and industrial projects for revenue. Anti-involution does not reform this.
3. **Scale:** The number of jurisdictions competing (hundreds of cities) and the capital involved dwarf any historical parallel.

*Conclusion of assessment:* Anti-involution is an administrative correction, not a structural reform. It may reduce destructive price wars and encourage some consolidation, but it does not address the root cause: insufficient domestic demand relative to production capacity. Without demand-side reform, the overcapacity problem is managed, not solved.

**Sources:** State Council (July 2025); NDRC unified market guideline (Jan 2025); World Bank CEU (Dec 2025); MERICS (2025); Klein and Pettis (2020) *Trade Wars Are Class Wars* (for demand-side framing).

### Section 4: Global Spillover — Downward Price Pressure (~700 words, ~1.3 pages)

**Purpose:** Trace the external consequence of the mechanism. Focus on China's export behavior and price transmission — not a survey of Western trade responses.

**Data domain:** Trade flow and export-price data only (domestic capacity data was in Section 2).

**Part A — Framing: China Shock 2.0 (~250 words):**
- Original China Shock (Autor, Dorn, Hanson 2013): labor-market effects of a large trade shock driven by genuine comparative advantage in labor-intensive manufacturing, amplified by WTO accession.
- Current wave: state-directed overcapacity in capital-intensive green tech. The mechanism is different (subsidized vs. market-driven), the scale is comparable, and the welfare calculus is more complex.
- **Key distinction:** Distinguish between (a) firms that built export-oriented capacity strategically (e.g., BYD's Thailand/Hungary plants) and (b) firms redirecting output abroad because domestic margins collapsed. These have different policy implications.

**Part B — Evidence of downward price pressure (~250 words):**
- Rising Chinese export volumes in clean-tech sectors accompanied by falling export unit values (cite USCC 2025 aggregates, IMF WP 2024/180 finding that subsidies promote exports and lower export prices).
- Note that RMB depreciation since 2022 amplifies price competitiveness (one sentence).
- Use precise language: "downward price pressure" — not "exported deflation," which implies a general price-level phenomenon rather than sector-specific terms-of-trade effects.

**Part C — The green-public-goods counterargument (~200 words):**
This is the strongest objection to the overcapacity-as-problem framing and must be engaged seriously. Cheap Chinese solar panels and batteries accelerate decarbonization globally, particularly in developing countries that cannot afford unsubsidized clean tech. Subsidies on goods with positive externalities may be globally welfare-improving.

*Response:* The welfare argument is real but incomplete. (1) Adjustment costs fall on specific sectors and workers in importing countries, creating concentrated losers even when aggregate welfare improves. (2) Dependence on a single subsidized source creates supply-chain vulnerability. (3) Trade policy responses (US 100% EV tariff, EU duties 17.4-37.6%) are already fragmenting markets, potentially raising costs for everyone — the political economy of the response undermines the welfare gain.

**Sources:** USCC Chapter 8 (2025); IMF WP 2024/180; EU Parliament study (2026); Fang, Li & Lu (2025) on export implications.

### Section 5: Tensions and Outlook (~600 words, ~1.1 pages)

**Purpose:** Synthesize and restate the thesis. One scenario developed, not two.

**The central tension (~200 words):**
Anti-involution addresses the symptom (too many firms competing destructively on price) but not the disease (too little domestic demand to absorb production). China needs both production-side discipline AND demand-side reform: household consumption growth, social protection expansion, local fiscal restructuring away from investment dependence. Anti-involution delivers only the first.

**Most likely outcome (~200 words):**
The most likely trajectory is a muddled middle — not clean success or failure. Partial consolidation in some sectors (solar likely to see exits first), continued overcapacity in others (EVs, where local government stakes are highest), differentiated trade responses across jurisdictions, and ongoing tension between domestic political economy and external adjustment. The green-transition benefit and the industrial-disruption cost will coexist uncomfortably.

**Thesis restated (~200 words):**
Anti-involution reveals the contradiction at the heart of China's contemporary growth model. The same state apparatus that created globally competitive green-manufacturing sectors through industrial policy now tries to regulate the overcapacity it produced. This is reform within the model, not retreat from it. Whether it succeeds depends less on administrative discipline and more on whether China can shift from production maximization toward a growth model anchored in domestic consumption, productivity, and sustainable demand. Until then, the global economy absorbs the spillover.

**Sources:** Minimal — this section synthesizes rather than introduces new evidence.
