# AGENTS.md

## Purpose

This file provides writing instructions for producing an economics-top-journal-style literature review on:

**Artificial Intelligence and the Economy: Productivity, Macroeconomic Adjustment, Financial Stability, and Policy Challenges**

The target is a concise 5–6 page literature review suitable for a graduate economics course and written in the style expected by high-level economics journals such as the *American Economic Review*, *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, *Journal of Political Economy*, *Review of Economic Studies*, and *Journal of Economic Literature*.

The review should identify:

1. What recent economics research has learned about AI.
2. Where the literature disagrees.
3. What questions remain open.
4. How future research could proceed.

---

## Core Writing Principle

Write the review as a **synthesis of economic mechanisms**, not as a sequence of paper summaries.

Do not write:

> Paper A says X. Paper B says Y. Paper C says Z.

Write:

> The literature establishes X but remains divided on Y. Micro evidence supports mechanism A, while macro models emphasize aggregation channel B. This gap between task-level productivity and aggregate welfare is the central unresolved issue.

---

## Target Standard

The review should read like a compressed *Journal of Economic Literature* discussion, not like an annotated bibliography.

A strong review must:

- Motivate the topic clearly.
- Organize papers into analytical strands.
- Identify the main contributions of core papers.
- Distinguish established findings from unresolved claims.
- Explain tensions and disagreements.
- End with open questions and research directions.
- Use precise, non-journalistic economics writing.
- Follow author-date citation conventions.

---

## Literature Review Philosophy

Use three rules.

### Rule 1: Be broad in bibliography, selective in exposition

A 5–6 page review may include 25–30 references, but it cannot discuss all papers equally. Use:

- 10–12 core papers for substantive discussion.
- 10–12 secondary papers for support, contrast, or extension.
- 5–6 background/policy sources for motivation or open questions.

Every cited paper must serve a clear function.

### Rule 2: Organize by mechanisms, not by chronology

The recommended structure is:

1. AI as a productivity technology.
2. AI and labor-market exposure.
3. Macro growth, output, investment, and inflation.
4. AI in finance and central banking.
5. AI as a research technology for economists.
6. Policy, distribution, taxation, and externalities.

Chronology matters only when it clarifies how the literature evolved.

### Rule 3: Make tensions explicit

A top-level literature review should not merely report agreement. It should identify where the literature is unsettled:

- Micro gains versus aggregate productivity.
- Exposure versus displacement.
- Automation versus augmentation.
- Productivity gains versus inequality.
- Market efficiency versus systemic fragility.
- AI adoption versus AI governance.
- Innovation benefits versus environmental costs.

---

## Evidence Hierarchy

When deciding what to cite and how much weight to assign, use this hierarchy:

1. Peer-reviewed economics journals: *QJE*, *JEL*, *JPE*, *AER*, *ReStud*, *JEEA*, *AEJ*.
2. NBER, CEPR, BIS, IMF, FSB working papers and reports.
3. Books from reputable academic or professional presses.
4. Official data/report sources such as Stanford AI Index.
5. SSRN/arXiv only when the paper is highly relevant, widely cited, or has no better published substitute.

Do not rely on blog posts, media articles, or corporate white papers for central claims unless they are used only for background motivation.

---

## Required Argument Structure

The review should have one central argument:

> The economics literature has established credible task-level and workplace evidence that AI can raise productivity, but macroeconomic and financial implications remain uncertain because they depend on adoption, complementary investment, organizational change, labor-market adjustment, market structure, financial-system feedbacks, and policy institutions.

Each section should advance this argument.

---

## Recommended Section Blueprint

### 1. Introduction

Tasks:

- State why AI is an economics topic.
- State the review question.
- State the core thesis.
- Preview the structure.

Do not begin with vague statements such as:

> Artificial intelligence has become increasingly important in recent years.

Instead, begin analytically:

> Artificial intelligence matters for economics because it changes the cost of prediction, the productivity of cognitive tasks, and the organization of information-intensive markets.

The introduction should not be a literature dump. It should frame the puzzle.

---

### 2. Micro Productivity and Labor-Market Exposure

Core papers:

- Noy and Zhang (2023)
- Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond (2025)
- Eloundou et al. (2024)
- Felten, Raj, and Seamans (2023)
- Autor (2024)
- Acemoglu and Restrepo (2020)

Required synthesis:

- Generative AI raises productivity in some tasks.
- Effects are heterogeneous across workers.
- Exposure is not the same as adoption or displacement.
- Older automation evidence warns that labor-saving technologies can reduce employment and wages.
- AI may either substitute for workers or augment expertise depending on deployment.

A strong paragraph should state the bottom line first, then cite evidence.

Example structure:

> The strongest evidence on AI is microeconomic rather than macroeconomic. Experimental and workplace studies find sizable productivity gains in specific cognitive tasks, especially writing and customer support. Exposure studies broaden this finding by showing that many occupations contain tasks potentially affected by LLMs, but exposure measures do not identify whether firms will automate, augment, or reorganize work. This distinction matters because automation technologies have historically reduced employment and wages when substitution dominates.

---

### 3. Macroeconomic Effects: Growth, Output, Investment, and Inflation

Core papers:

- Acemoglu (2024)
- Aldasoro et al. (2024)
- Gambacorta et al. (2026)
- Aghion, Jones, and Jones (2018)
- Brynjolfsson, Rock, and Syverson (2018, 2021)
- Aghion and Bunel (2024)

Required synthesis:

- Macro effects are the main unresolved area.
- Acemoglu provides the cautious benchmark.
- Aldasoro et al. emphasize multi-sector general equilibrium channels.
- Gambacorta et al. add cross-country heterogeneity.
- Productivity-paradox and J-curve papers explain why measured gains may be delayed.
- Growth-theory papers connect AI to automation and long-run growth.

Key tension:

> Micro productivity gains do not mechanically imply large aggregate gains.

Explain why:

- Only some tasks are affected.
- Adoption is costly.
- Firms need complementary intangible capital.
- Market power may affect diffusion.
- Labor reallocation may be slow.
- AI investment raises demand as well as supply.
- Inflation effects are ambiguous.

---

### 4. Finance, Capital Markets, and Central Banking

Core papers and sources:

- IMF GFSR (2024), Chapter 3
- Aldasoro et al. (2024), Intelligent Financial System
- Adrian, Mosk, and Wu (2025)
- BIS (2024)
- FSB (2024)

Required synthesis:

- AI improves information processing and risk management.
- AI also creates systemic vulnerabilities.
- Central banks face AI both as users and regulators.
- Financial stability questions deserve separate attention from productivity questions.

Key tension:

> AI may make markets more informationally efficient but more fragile.

Mechanisms:

- Faster trading and information processing.
- More similar models across institutions.
- Third-party dependence on AI providers.
- Opacity and explainability problems.
- Cyber and operational risk.
- Herding and correlated strategies.
- Regulatory lag.

---

### 5. AI as a Research Technology for Economists

Core papers:

- Korinek (2023)
- Dell (2025)
- Fernández-Villaverde (2025)
- Chi et al. (2025)

Required synthesis:

- AI changes the research production function.
- Generative AI assists writing, coding, data cleaning, ideation, and derivations.
- Deep learning allows economists to use unstructured text and image data at scale.
- Machine learning can improve forecasting when validation is rigorous.
- Deep learning may help solve high-dimensional models.

Key caution:

> AI tools reduce some research costs but do not replace identification, measurement, or economic reasoning.

Discuss:

- Reproducibility.
- Hallucination.
- Data leakage.
- Interpretability.
- Validation.
- Disclosure norms.
- Replication packages.

---

### 6. Policy Challenges and Open Questions

Core papers:

- Korinek (2024)
- Thuemmel (2023)
- Bonfiglioli et al. (2025)
- BIS (2024)
- IMF (2024)
- FSB (2024)

Required synthesis:

- Policy questions are less settled than productivity questions.
- AI may raise welfare and inequality at the same time.
- Taxation, education, competition, and financial regulation all matter.
- Environmental costs are part of the welfare calculus.

Open questions must be concrete:

- How large are aggregate productivity gains once adoption costs and intangibles are included?
- Which workers are displaced, which are augmented, and over what horizon?
- Should AI be taxed as capital, regulated as infrastructure, or treated as a general-purpose technology?
- How should antitrust policy treat data, compute, and foundation-model concentration?
- Can financial regulators monitor AI-driven market correlations and cyber risk?
- How should AI-related electricity demand and emissions be priced?
- Will AI widen income gaps between advanced and emerging economies?

## Paragraph-Level Rules

Follow these rules for every paragraph:

1. Start with a topic sentence that states the claim.
2. Use citations as evidence, not as the sentence’s main content.
3. Do not summarize more than two or three papers in one paragraph unless they share the same mechanism.
4. End with the implication, tension, or transition.
5. Keep paragraphs short: 4–6 sentences.
6. Use present tense for literature: “Acemoglu (2024) argues,” “Dell (2025) shows.”
7. Avoid “clearly,” “obviously,” “very,” and “striking” unless the evidence itself makes the point.
8. Do not use footnotes as a dumping ground for papers that do not fit the argument.

---

## Citation Rules

Use AEA/Chicago author-date style.

Examples:

- Loud citation: `Acemoglu (2024) argues that...`
- Soft citation: `This view is consistent with task-based models of automation (Acemoglu 2024; Aghion, Jones, and Jones 2018).`
- Three or more authors in text after first mention: `Brynjolfsson et al. (2025) find...`

Rules:

- Every in-text citation must appear in the reference list.
- Every reference-list entry must be cited in the text.
- Prefer formal citations to descriptive references.
- Do not write: “In an interesting article by MIT professor Daron Acemoglu...”
- Write: “Acemoglu (2024) provides a task-based benchmark...”

---

## Reference-List Rules

Use AEA-style entries where possible.

Journal article template:

`Author Last Name, First Name, Second Author First Name Last Name, and Third Author First Name Last Name. Year. “Title.” Journal Volume (Issue): pages.`

Working paper template:

`Author Last Name, First Name. Year. “Title.” Institution Working Paper No. ####.`

Policy report template:

`Institution. Year. “Title.” Report/Chapter title. URL.`

Book template:

`Author Last Name, First Name, Second Author First Name Last Name, and Third Author First Name Last Name. Year. Title. City: Publisher.`

---

## Source-Selection Rules

Include a paper only if it performs one of these functions:

1. Establishes a core fact.
2. Provides a model.
3. Measures exposure or adoption.
4. Supplies evidence on productivity or labor effects.
5. Provides a macro or financial-stability mechanism.
6. Helps explain a disagreement.
7. Motivates an open question.
8. Provides a policy implication.

Remove papers that merely repeat an already established point.

---

## Common Failure Modes

Avoid these mistakes:

1. **Annotated bibliography:** one paragraph per paper.
2. **Overclaiming:** treating task-level gains as aggregate GDP gains.
3. **Policy essay tone:** making normative claims without welfare trade-offs.
4. **Technology hype:** presenting AI as automatically transformative.
5. **Excessive skepticism:** ignoring credible micro evidence.
6. **Citation padding:** citing papers that are not used in the argument.
7. **No hierarchy:** treating a JEL article, a working paper, and a background report equally.
8. **No mechanism:** listing findings without explaining why they differ.
9. **No open questions:** ending with a generic conclusion instead of a research frontier.
10. **Unclear scope:** mixing “AI as technology,” “AI as macro shock,” and “AI as research tool” without transitions.

---

## Required Final Review Structure

Use the following structure unless the instructor requires otherwise:

1. Introduction
2. Micro Productivity and Labor-Market Exposure
3. Macroeconomic Adjustment: Growth, Output, Investment, and Inflation
4. AI in Finance and Central Banking
5. AI as a Research Technology for Economists
6. Policy Challenges and Open Questions
7. Conclusion
8. References

---

## Quality-Control Checklist

Before submitting, verify:

- The introduction states a clear thesis.
- The review has 25–30 references but discusses only 10–12 in depth.
- Each section has a bottom-line claim.
- The review distinguishes evidence from speculation.
- The macro section explains why aggregation is hard.
- The finance section explains both benefits and systemic risks.
- The policy section identifies concrete open questions.
- Citations are in author-date style.
- The reference list is alphabetized.
- Every citation has a reference-list entry.
- Every reference-list entry is cited.
- No paragraph is merely a paper summary.
- No sentence uses hype language without evidence.
- The conclusion answers: what has been learned and what remains unresolved.

---

## Recommended Working Thesis for Drafting

Use this as the anchor for the final review:

> The recent economics literature on AI has established credible task-level productivity effects and has begun to model AI’s macroeconomic and financial channels. Yet its central unresolved problem is aggregation: economists still do not know how task-level gains translate into aggregate productivity, inflation, labor-market adjustment, financial stability, and welfare once adoption costs, intangible investment, market structure, policy institutions, and environmental externalities are taken seriously.

---

## Recommended One-Paragraph Abstract

Artificial intelligence has become a central topic in economics because it affects cognitive labor, prediction, firm organization, financial markets, and the production of economic research. This review synthesizes recent work on AI and productivity, macroeconomic adjustment, finance, and policy. The literature provides credible evidence that generative AI can raise productivity in specific tasks and workplaces, while exposure studies suggest that many occupations may be affected. However, macroeconomic models disagree on the size and timing of aggregate gains because adoption requires complementary investment, reorganization, and sectoral reallocation. AI also creates a financial-stability trade-off: it may improve information processing and risk management while increasing opacity, model herding, cyber risk, and third-party dependence. The main research frontier is to connect micro evidence to aggregate outcomes and to design policies that manage inequality, financial fragility, competition, and environmental costs without suppressing innovation.

---

## Sentence Templates

Use these templates to keep the writing analytical.

### To introduce a literature strand

`A first strand of the literature studies [mechanism], showing that [bottom-line finding].`

### To contrast papers

`This conclusion differs from [paper] because [mechanism/model/data difference].`

### To connect micro and macro evidence

`The central challenge is that [micro finding] does not directly identify [macro outcome].`

### To state a tension

`The literature therefore faces a tension between [benefit] and [risk].`

### To identify a gap

`What remains unresolved is not whether [narrow result], but whether [broader economic mechanism].`

### To open the conclusion

`The literature has advanced most clearly on [known result], but remains unsettled on [open issue].`

---

## Final Instruction

Write like an economist explaining a research frontier to impatient economists. Be generous with citations, but ruthless in exposition. The purpose is not to prove that many papers were read; the purpose is to show what the literature has taught us, where it disagrees, and why the next research questions matter.
