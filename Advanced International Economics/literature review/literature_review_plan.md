# Literature Review Plan

**Working title:** Artificial Intelligence and the Economy: Productivity, Macroeconomic Adjustment, Financial Stability, and Policy Challenges  
**Course task:** Write a concise 5–6 page literature review on recent advances in a topic, identifying what has been learned and what challenges remain.  
**Prepared date:** 2026-05-22  
**Recommended bibliography size:** 25–30 references total.  
**Recommended discussion depth:** 10–12 core papers discussed substantively; 15–18 additional papers used selectively.

---

## 1. One-Sentence Thesis

Recent economics research shows that artificial intelligence can raise productivity in specific cognitive tasks and may reshape labor markets, macroeconomic dynamics, finance, and economic research itself, but the literature remains unsettled on how task-level gains aggregate into long-run growth, inflation, inequality, financial stability, and welfare.

---

## 2. Review Question

**Main question:** What have economists learned about the economic effects of artificial intelligence, and what are the main unresolved challenges for macroeconomic adjustment, financial stability, and policy?

**Sub-questions:**

1. What does micro-level evidence show about AI and productivity?
2. How do macroeconomic models translate task-level gains into output, investment, employment, and inflation?
3. Does AI mainly complement workers, substitute for labor, or reorganize production?
4. How does AI transform finance, capital markets, and central banking?
5. How is AI changing economic research itself?
6. What policy problems remain unresolved: inequality, taxation, competition, financial regulation, environmental costs, and global divergence?

---

## 3. Core Argument

The review should not claim that AI will mechanically generate large macroeconomic gains. Instead, it should present a structured tension:

- **Micro evidence is relatively strong:** task-level and workplace studies show meaningful productivity gains.
- **Macro evidence is still uncertain:** aggregate effects depend on which tasks are exposed, adoption speed, complementary intangible investment, sectoral reallocation, market power, and general equilibrium feedbacks.
- **Finance is a double-edged area:** AI can improve information processing and risk management but may also amplify opacity, model herding, cyber risks, and third-party dependence.
- **Policy remains underdeveloped:** the literature has not converged on how to regulate AI-driven labor displacement, market concentration, data/compute access, financial instability, or emissions from AI infrastructure.

---

## 4. Suggested Structure for a 5–6 Page Review

### Introduction: Why AI is an economic topic, not only a technology topic
**Length:** 0.5 page  
**Main purpose:** Motivate the topic and state the review’s synthesis claim.

Use AI adoption and investment facts to motivate relevance, but keep the opening analytical rather than journalistic. The introduction should state that AI matters because it changes the cost of prediction, cognitive labor, firm organization, capital-market information processing, and economic research methods.

**Papers to cite:** BIS (2024), Stanford HAI (2025), Korinek (2024), Brynjolfsson, Korinek, and Agrawal (2025).

**Possible topic sentence:**  
Artificial intelligence has become a central economic issue because it affects both production and information processing: it can automate cognitive tasks, alter labor demand, reshape financial markets, and change how economists produce knowledge.

---

### Section 1: Micro Productivity Evidence and Labor-Market Exposure
**Length:** 1 page  
**Main purpose:** Establish what is relatively well known.

**Main claim:** The most credible evidence so far is micro-level: AI improves productivity in specific tasks and workplaces, but exposure does not automatically imply job replacement.

**Core papers:**

- Noy and Zhang (2023): experimental evidence on writing tasks.
- Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond (2025): workplace evidence from customer support.
- Eloundou et al. (2024): task-level exposure of work to LLMs.
- Felten, Raj, and Seamans (2023): occupational heterogeneity in exposure.
- Autor (2024): possibility that AI could rebuild middle-class expertise if deployed as augmentation.
- Acemoglu and Restrepo (2020): background evidence from automation/robots as a cautionary labor-market benchmark.

**What has been learned:**

- AI can improve speed and quality in defined cognitive tasks.
- Gains may be larger for novice or lower-performing workers in some settings.
- Occupational exposure is broad but heterogeneous.
- The distinction between exposure, adoption, productivity, and displacement is essential.

**Remaining challenges:**

- Micro studies are often task- or firm-specific.
- The welfare effect depends on wages, labor displacement, task creation, and who captures productivity gains.
- The literature still lacks enough evidence on long-run worker learning, firm reorganization, and occupational mobility.

---

### Section 2: Macroeconomic Effects: Growth, Output, Investment, and Inflation
**Length:** 1.25 pages  
**Main purpose:** Make this the intellectual center of the review.

**Main claim:** The macro literature disagrees less about whether AI raises productivity somewhere than about how large, fast, and broadly distributed the aggregate effects will be.

**Core papers:**

- Acemoglu (2024): cautious task-based benchmark.
- Aldasoro et al. (2024): multi-sector macro model of output and inflation.
- Gambacorta et al. (2026): heterogeneous growth effects across advanced and emerging economies.
- Aghion, Jones, and Jones (2018): growth-theory approach to AI as automation.
- Brynjolfsson, Rock, and Syverson (2018): AI and the modern productivity paradox.
- Brynjolfsson, Rock, and Syverson (2021): Productivity J-curve and intangible complements.
- Aghion and Bunel (2024): comparative assessment of macro-growth estimates.

**What has been learned:**

- A task-based approach disciplines exaggerated claims about aggregate GDP effects.
- AI can raise output, consumption, and investment in general equilibrium models.
- Inflation effects are ambiguous: AI raises supply through productivity but can raise demand through investment and income effects.
- Adoption lags and intangible investment can delay measured productivity gains.
- AI may widen cross-country gaps if advanced economies adopt earlier and have stronger AI preparedness.

**Remaining challenges:**

- The mapping from task productivity to aggregate TFP is not settled.
- Measured productivity may understate true gains during the investment phase.
- Distribution across sectors, occupations, firms, and countries remains central.
- Existing models still simplify data, compute, market power, and governance constraints.

---

### Section 3: Finance, Capital Markets, and Central Banking
**Length:** 1 page  
**Main purpose:** Give the review a macro-financial dimension.

**Main claim:** AI may improve capital-market efficiency and financial intermediation, but the same features can amplify systemic risk.

**Core papers and policy sources:**

- IMF (2024), Global Financial Stability Report, Chapter 3.
- Aldasoro et al. (2024), Intelligent Financial System.
- Adrian, Mosk, and Wu (2025).
- BIS (2024).
- FSB (2024).

**What has been learned:**

- AI can process unstructured data, automate financial analysis, improve risk management, and support supervision.
- AI may improve liquidity and capital-market efficiency.
- Financial firms are adopting AI in intermediation, asset management, insurance, payments, and compliance.

**Remaining challenges:**

- AI may generate model herding, correlated trading, opacity, cyber vulnerabilities, third-party dependence, and concentration of infrastructure.
- Regulators face a speed problem: AI may change markets faster than reporting, supervision, and stress-testing frameworks adapt.
- The interaction between AI valuations, market concentration, and macro-financial risk is still underdeveloped.

---

### Section 4: AI as a Research Technology for Economists
**Length:** 0.75 page  
**Main purpose:** Show that AI changes both the economy and economics as a discipline.

**Main claim:** AI is not only an object of study; it is becoming a research input that changes data construction, forecasting, model solution, coding, and writing.

**Core papers:**

- Korinek (2023): generative AI for economic research.
- Dell (2025): deep learning for economists.
- Fernández-Villaverde (2025): deep learning for solving economic models.
- Chi et al. (2025): macroeconomic forecasting and machine learning.

**What has been learned:**

- Generative AI can help economists with ideation, writing, coding, data analysis, and mathematical derivation.
- Deep learning makes large-scale unstructured text and image data more usable in empirical economics.
- Machine learning can improve macroeconomic forecasting when models use regularization, validation, and nonlinearities carefully.
- Deep learning may relax computational constraints in high-dimensional economic models.

**Remaining challenges:**

- Reproducibility, hallucination, interpretability, data leakage, and validation standards remain central.
- The discipline needs norms for using AI tools without undermining transparency.
- AI-assisted empirical work must still satisfy economic identification and measurement standards.

---

### Section 5: Policy Challenges, Distribution, Taxation, and Externalities
**Length:** 1 page  
**Main purpose:** Synthesize the unresolved frontier.

**Main claim:** The policy literature is still less settled than the productivity literature.

**Core papers:**

- Korinek (2024): broad policy challenges.
- Thuemmel (2023): optimal taxation of robots.
- Bonfiglioli et al. (2025): environmental cost of AI.
- BIS (2024), IMF (2024), FSB (2024): policy and financial regulation.

**What has been learned:**

- AI can intensify inequality if gains accrue mainly to capital owners, high-skill workers, or firms controlling data and compute.
- Taxation of automation is theoretically possible but not straightforward; labor and capital taxation interact with robot/AI adoption.
- AI infrastructure may increase emissions through data centers and electricity demand.
- AI governance is both domestic and international because technology, finance, and data flows cross borders.

**Remaining challenges:**

- How should policy balance innovation and redistribution?
- Should AI-specific taxes, robot taxes, or broader income/capital tax reforms be preferred?
- How should competition policy treat compute, data, foundation models, and platform concentration?
- How can financial regulators monitor AI-driven systemic risk?
- How should environmental policy account for data-center energy use?

---

### Conclusion
**Length:** 0.5 page  
**Main purpose:** Answer the assignment prompt directly.

**Suggested synthesis:**  
The literature has made clear progress in documenting AI’s task-level productivity effects and in modeling possible macroeconomic and financial channels. Yet the central unresolved issue is aggregation: economists still do not know how quickly micro-level improvements will translate into aggregate productivity, how gains will be distributed, and whether AI will stabilize or destabilize financial and labor markets. Future work must connect micro evidence, macro modeling, financial stability, and policy design more directly.

## 6. How to Use the 28 Papers Without Overcrowding the Review

### Core discussion hierarchy

- **Must discuss carefully:** Acemoglu (2024); Aldasoro et al. (2024 output/inflation); BIS (2024); IMF (2024); Aldasoro et al. (2024 finance); Korinek (2023, 2024); Dell (2025); Noy and Zhang (2023); Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond (2025).
- **Use as conceptual bridges:** Aghion, Jones, and Jones (2018); Brynjolfsson, Rock, and Syverson (2018, 2021); Aghion and Bunel (2024); Agrawal, Gans, and Goldfarb (2018).
- **Use as targeted support:** Eloundou et al. (2024); Felten et al. (2023); Autor (2024); Acemoglu and Restrepo (2020); Fernández-Villaverde (2025); Chi et al. (2025); Adrian et al. (2025); Thuemmel (2023); Bonfiglioli et al. (2025); Stanford HAI (2025); FSB (2024).

### Avoid this style

> Noy and Zhang study writing tasks. Brynjolfsson et al. study customer support. Eloundou et al. study occupational exposure. Felten et al. study occupational exposure...

### Use this style instead

> The micro evidence is more mature than the macro evidence. Experimental and workplace studies show that generative AI can raise productivity in specific cognitive tasks, especially writing and customer support (Noy and Zhang 2023; Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond 2025). Exposure-based studies broaden this evidence by showing that LLMs can affect a large set of work tasks, although exposure is not equivalent to automation or displacement (Eloundou et al. 2024; Felten, Raj, and Seamans 2023). This distinction matters because older automation evidence shows that labor-saving technologies can reduce employment and wages when substitution dominates (Acemoglu and Restrepo 2020), while more optimistic work argues that AI could rebuild middle-skill expertise if used as an augmentation technology (Autor 2024).

## 7. Suggested Opening Paragraph

Artificial intelligence has become a central topic in economics because it affects both production and information processing. Unlike earlier automation technologies that primarily substituted for routine physical or clerical tasks, recent generative AI systems can perform cognitive tasks, process unstructured data, assist decision-making, and reshape the organization of firms and financial markets. The recent economics literature has therefore moved quickly from documenting task-level productivity gains to asking whether AI will alter aggregate growth, inflation, labor-market inequality, financial stability, and the research process itself. This review argues that the literature has established meaningful micro-level productivity effects, but has not yet resolved how these gains aggregate into macroeconomic outcomes or how policy should respond to the resulting distributional, financial, and environmental risks.

---

## 8. Suggested Concluding Paragraph

The literature on AI and the economy has advanced rapidly, but its central conclusion remains conditional rather than definitive. Micro-level studies show that AI can raise productivity in specific tasks and workplaces, while exposure studies suggest that many occupations may be affected. Macro models, however, disagree on the size, speed, and distribution of aggregate gains because adoption requires complementary investment, organizational change, and sectoral reallocation. The financial literature similarly identifies both efficiency gains and systemic risks. The main frontier is therefore aggregation and governance: future work must connect task-level evidence to firm behavior, labor-market adjustment, capital-market dynamics, environmental externalities, and the policy institutions needed to distribute AI’s gains while limiting its risks.

---

## 9. Writing Checklist

- The introduction should answer: why AI, why economics, why now?
- Do not write one paragraph per paper.
- Group papers by claims, mechanisms, and unresolved tensions.
- Use author-date citations in AEA style.
- Make every paper serve a function: theory, evidence, measurement, policy, or open question.
- The review should distinguish what is known from what is speculative.
- The open-questions section should not be a generic list; it should follow from the tensions identified earlier.
- Prefer peer-reviewed economics journals, NBER/CEPR/BIS/IMF working papers, and official policy reports.
- Use Stanford AI Index only for motivation and background, not as a substitute for economics literature.
- Keep the review concise: 5–6 pages should contain synthesis, not exhaustive summaries.
